def equilibrium(xco2, temperature=TEMPERATURE_C, salinity=SALINITY):

Temperature (°C) pH Change from 15 °C

The direct pH effect is small at fixed TA and atmospheric xCO₂. Changes in CO₂ solubility and acid–base equilibria partly offset one another while DIC adjusts. f) Aragonite and calcite saturation¶

Question — part f

At the exercise's present-day CO₂ value of 430 ppm, calculate the saturation states of aragonite and calcite, two forms of CaCO₃ used by marine calcifiers. Interpret the values relative to a saturation state of one.

# Your calculation

target_aragonite = float(present['saturation_aragonite'])
print(f"Present Omega_aragonite = {target_aragonite:.3f}")
print(f"Present Omega_calcite = {float(present['saturation_calcite']):.3f}")

Present Omega_aragonite = 1.975
Present Omega_calcite = 3.075

Instructor answer

$\Omega_{arag}=1.975$ and $\Omega_{calc}=3.075$. Saturation state is $\Omega=[Ca^{2+}][CO_3^{2-}]/K_{sp}$ for the specified mineral. Both values exceed one, so the water is supersaturated with respect to both minerals. Below one it is undersaturated. Supersaturation describes the chemical driving force; it does not alone establish a calcification rate or guarantee healthy organisms. Aragonite has the lower saturation state in this calculation.

Higher CO₂ increases DIC, lowers pH by about 0.302, and reduces carbonate availability and aragonite saturation. It therefore has a small direct pH effect here, but a noticeable saturation effect. Both future states are still supersaturated with respect to aragonite, although less so than the present.

The endpoint is prescribed, not a new forecast or a transient model result. The small pH sensitivity does not make warming harmless: heat stress can cause coral bleaching, which this chemistry calculation does not model (NOAA: coral bleaching).

Question — part h

# Your calculation

oae_states = {}
for temperature in (15.0, 18.0):

Both states reproduce the target aragonite saturation of 1.975 by construction. For the main 15 °C answer, TA must rise by about 39%. The warmer alternative requires less TA to attain the same saturation state. Neither restores present pH (7.982): saturation and pH are different properties, and restoring one does not fix the other.

The target saturation is prescribed, and TA is inferred. The final assertions check the chemistry inversion; they do not independently validate alkalinity enhancement as a reef intervention. DIC rises as the water equilibrates with the prescribed atmosphere. There is no closed ocean–atmosphere carbon inventory here, and this is a concentration increment, not a total dose or addition rate. A dose would require the treated water mass and a specified material; adding carbonate mineral also adds DIC, unlike an idealized pure-TA addition.

Higher equilibrium DIC is consistent with potential CO₂ uptake, but these states do not quantify uptake rates, net atmospheric removal, mixing, precipitation, cost or ecological feasibility. Restored saturation also does not remove thermal stress or guarantee coral survival. A coupled process model and site-specific evidence would be needed to assess an intervention.
